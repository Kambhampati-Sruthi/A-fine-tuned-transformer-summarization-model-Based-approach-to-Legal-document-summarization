ABSTRACT
This report presents the learning outcomes and project work undertaken as part of the "Advanced Robotics Using AI and IoT" Virtual Internship, offered by EduSkills Foundation in collaboration with AICTE (All India Council for Technical Education) under the National Internship Portal, Ministry of Education. The 8-week internship, delivered through an online learning management platform, was designed to equip students with foundational and applied knowledge of robotics, Artificial Intelligence (AI), and the Internet of Things (IoT), aimed at building intelligent, connected, and autonomous robotic systems.
The program combined theoretical concepts with hands-on practical implementation using tools such as Arduino Uno, Raspberry Pi, Python, embedded C, sensors (ultrasonic, PIR, DHT11), actuators (DC motors, servos), the Robot Operating System (ROS)/Gazebo simulation environment, and the MQTT protocol for IoT communication. Key learning areas included robotics fundamentals and terminology, basic electronics, microcontroller programming, sensor and actuator interfacing, robot kinematics and motion control, AI and machine learning fundamentals for perception, IoT communication protocols, data acquisition and processing, robot simulation, and ethical and safety considerations in robotics.
The internship culminated in a capstone project titled "Smart IoT-Enabled Security & Navigation Robot", an integrated hardware–software system comprising two cooperating modules — a Navigation System (Arduino-based obstacle avoidance and path following using ultrasonic sensing) and a Perception & Alerting System (Raspberry Pi-based motion detection, image capture, and MQTT-based cloud alerting). The project demonstrates the practical integration of embedded electronics, AI-assisted perception, and IoT connectivity to solve a real-world security and monitoring problem, bridging the gap between academic knowledge and industry-oriented robotics engineering practice. The program concluded with a certification upon successful completion of all module assessments, validating both the learning progress and the technical competency achieved during the internship.

INTRODUCTION TO INTERNSHIP TOPIC
Robotics is the interdisciplinary branch of engineering and computer science that deals with the design, construction, operation, and use of robots — machines capable of carrying out a series of actions autonomously or semi-autonomously. Modern robotics integrates mechanical design, electronics, embedded programming, Artificial Intelligence, and networked connectivity to create systems that can sense their environment, make decisions, and act upon the physical world.
Historical and Conceptual Background of Robotics
The Module 1 material covered in the EduSkills course traced robotics back to its origins: the word “robot” itself comes from the Czech word “robota” (forced labour), coined by playwright Karel Čapek in 1920, though the underlying idea of artificial mechanical helpers goes back to ancient automatons and Leonardo da Vinci’s sketches of a mechanical knight. Modern robotics, however, really began in 1954, when George Devol patented the Unimate, the first industrial robotic arm, which was installed on a General Motors assembly line in 1961 to lift and stack hot metal die-castings. Learning this history was useful context for the internship: it framed today’s AI- and IoT-connected robots such as the capstone project’s Navigation and Perception & Alerting modules as the latest step in a decades-long progression — from purely mechanical, pre-programmed “pick-and-place” arms, through microprocessor-controlled robots in the 1970s–80s, to today’s sensor-rich, network-connected, AI-assisted systems used in manufacturing, healthcare, agriculture, and space and underwater exploration.
A robotic system is typically built from four core building blocks: sensors (to perceive the environment), actuators (to produce motion or physical action), a controller/microcontroller (to process information and make decisions), and a power system. When these components are combined with Artificial Intelligence and Internet of Things (IoT) connectivity, robots evolve from simple pre-programmed machines into intelligent, connected systems capable of learning, adapting, and communicating with remote users and cloud platforms.
Role of AI in Robotics
Artificial Intelligence enables robots to move beyond fixed, rule-based behaviour toward perception, reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. In this internship, AI concepts were applied to robotic perception — for example, using Machine Learning fundamentals to help a robot interpret sensor data (such as distinguishing a genuine obstacle from sensor noise, or recognising a motion event as significant) and to make basic autonomous decisions such as path re-routing or triggering an alert.
Role of IoT in Robotics
The Internet of Things extends a robot's capability beyond its own local sensors and actuators by connecting it to the internet and cloud services. Using lightweight, publish/subscribe protocols such as MQTT, a robot can transmit sensor readings, status updates, and alerts to a remote broker, which in turn can notify a user's dashboard or mobile device in real time. This internship focused on MQTT as the primary IoT communication protocol, owing to its low overhead, reliability, and suitability for resource-constrained embedded devices such as the Arduino and Raspberry Pi.
Why an Integrated AI + IoT + Robotics Approach Matters
None of these three domains delivers a complete solution in isolation: mechanical and electronic hardware alone can only react to pre-programmed rules, Artificial Intelligence without sensors and actuators has no way to perceive or act on the physical world, and IoT connectivity without an intelligent controller is simply a data pipe with nothing meaningful to send. The capstone project in this internship was deliberately structured to combine all three: the Arduino handled deterministic, low-latency navigation and safety logic; the Raspberry Pi added AI-assisted perception; and MQTT-based IoT connectivity tied both modules together and exposed the robot's status to a remote dashboard. This integrated approach reflects how production robotics systems are actually engineered in industry, which made the internship’s project-based format particularly valuable preparation for real-world embedded and AI system design.

MODULES
The course content was delivered through 15 structured modules, each comprising a conceptual lesson, a practical implementation exercise, real-world examples, and an assessment. These modules are summarised below, followed by a detailed description of the two integration modules developed for the capstone project.
Course Modules (1–15)
Detailed Module Descriptions
Each module below combines a conceptual lesson, a hands-on practical implementation exercise, real-world case studies, and a graded assessment, forming a complete, self-paced learning unit on the EduSkills platform.
Module 1: Introduction to Robotics Concepts & Terminology
This foundational module introduced the definition, history, and classification of robots (industrial, mobile, humanoid, and service robots), along with core terminology such as degrees of freedom, autonomy, and teleoperation. The practical exercise involved identifying real-world robotic components and mapping them to their functional roles, followed by case studies of robots used across manufacturing, healthcare, and logistics. This grounding in classification and terminology later helped me communicate design decisions clearly during the capstone project's system-architecture discussions.
Module 2: Basic Electronics for Robotics
This module covered the fundamentals of voltage, current, resistance, and Ohm's Law, along with power dissipation calculations for selecting appropriately rated resistors. In the hands-on lab, I used a breadboard and jumper wires to assemble a basic LED circuit with a current-limiting resistor, building the foundational electronics skills required for all subsequent hardware modules. I also learned to read component datasheets and select resistor tolerances appropriate for prototype-grade circuits, a habit that reduced wiring errors later in the project.
Module 3: Introduction to Microcontrollers (Arduino / Raspberry Pi Pico)
This module introduced microcontroller architecture and the role of Arduino and Raspberry Pi Pico boards in robotics. I set up the Arduino IDE, learned core functions such as pinMode(), digitalWrite(), and analogRead(), and wrote and uploaded my first "Blink" sketch to control an onboard LED — the foundational exercise for all later embedded-programming work. This module also introduced serial-communication debugging using the Arduino IDE's Serial Monitor, a tool I relied on throughout the rest of the internship.
Module 4: Sensors in Robotics
This module explored the principles and applications of common robotic sensors, including ultrasonic distance sensors, PIR motion sensors, and analog sensors. The practical component involved interfacing the HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor with the Arduino to measure real-time distance readings, directly feeding into the Navigation module of the capstone project. I also compared sensor accuracy under different lighting and surface conditions, which helped me understand the practical limitations of low-cost sensing hardware.
Module 5: Actuators in Robotics
This module covered DC motors, servo motors, and H-bridge motor drivers (L298N), explaining how electrical signals are converted into physical motion. In the hands-on lab, I wired and controlled a DC motor's speed and direction using PWM signals through the motor driver, forming the locomotion backbone of the capstone robot. Understanding H-bridge polarity switching was essential for implementing smooth forward, reverse, and turning manoeuvres in the final robot chassis.
Module 6: Introduction to Programming for Robotics
This module built Python and C++ programming fundamentals — variables, control flow, functions, and loops — and demonstrated how this logic translates into robot behaviour. The practical exercise focused on writing structured, reusable code to control sensor-driven decisions, a skill directly applied in the navigation and perception logic of the final project. Practising modular function design also made later debugging and code reuse across the two capstone modules significantly easier.
Module 7: Basic Robot Kinematics and Motion Control
This module introduced differential-drive kinematics, explaining how independently controlled wheel speeds produce turning and forward motion. The practical exercise involved implementing simple path-following and obstacle-avoidance algorithms, which were later refined and integrated into the capstone project's Navigation module. Calculating turning radius from differential wheel speeds gave me a much clearer intuition for how software commands translate into physical robot movement.
Module 8: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence (AI) for Robotics
This module introduced core AI concepts — perception, reasoning, and decision-making — and how they apply to robotic systems. Through guided exercises, I explored how a robot can use sensor input to make simple autonomous decisions, forming the conceptual basis for the Perception & Alerting module of the capstone project. This module also introduced the distinction between rule-based logic and learned models, a distinction that shaped how I approached the Perception module's design.
Module 9: Machine Learning Fundamentals for Perception
This module covered the basics of supervised learning and how machine learning models can be applied to interpret sensor data — for example, distinguishing genuine motion events from noise. The practical exercise involved applying basic ML concepts to classify sensor readings, directly relevant to the AI&DS discipline and to the capstone project's perception logic. I also learned to evaluate a simple classifier using accuracy and false-positive rate, metrics that later informed how I tuned the motion-alert threshold.
Module 10: Introduction to the Internet of Things (IoT) for Robotics
This module introduced IoT architecture and the MQTT publish/subscribe protocol, explaining how lightweight messaging enables robots to communicate with cloud services and remote users. The hands-on lab involved setting up a Mosquitto broker and publishing/subscribing to topics — the exact mechanism used for alert delivery in the capstone project. I also compared MQTT with HTTP polling for this use case and confirmed that a lightweight publish/subscribe model was better suited to battery-powered, intermittently connected robots.
Module 11: Data Acquisition and Processing for Robotics
This module focused on acquiring, logging, and filtering sensor data to remove noise before it is used for decision-making — a core data-science skill directly relevant to my department. The practical exercise involved processing raw sensor readings and visualizing them to identify trends and anomalies. I also practised basic outlier detection on noisy ultrasonic readings, which improved the reliability of the obstacle-avoidance logic used in the capstone build.
Module 12: Robot Simulation and Virtual Environments
This module introduced the Gazebo simulator and the Robot Operating System (ROS), allowing robot behaviour to be tested virtually before physical deployment. In the hands-on lab, I installed and ran a Gazebo simulation to validate navigation logic in a safe, virtual environment prior to (conceptual) hardware deployment. Working in simulation first also helped me catch several logic errors early, before they could have caused hardware damage during physical testing.
Module 13: Ethical Considerations and Safety in Robotics
This module addressed responsible AI, data privacy, and safety protocols for robots operating around people. Through case studies, I examined real-world incidents involving robotic systems and derived safety guidelines that were later applied to the fail-safe design of the capstone project's Navigation module. This module also prompted me to add a manual override and a clearly documented shutdown procedure to the capstone robot's design.
Module 14: Project-Based Learning for Integrated Robotics
This module guided the ideation, scope definition, and system-architecture design of the capstone project, "Smart IoT-Enabled Security & Navigation Robot" Documenting these interfaces early made the subsequent integration of the Navigation and Perception modules considerably smoother.. I defined the two-module architecture (Navigation and Perception & Alerting), selected appropriate hardware and communication protocols, and documented the module interfaces before implementation.
Module 15: Troubleshooting & Final Assessment
This final module covered a systematic approach to debugging hardware/software integration issues, using tools such as the Arduino Serial Monitor and MQTT Explorer. I applied these techniques to resolve sensor-timing and connectivity issues in the capstone project before completing the Final Assessment and receiving certification. This module reinforced the value of systematic, one-variable-at-a-time debugging over guesswork when resolving intermittent hardware faults.
Capstone Project Modules — "Smart IoT-Enabled Security & Navigation Robot"
Module 1: Navigation System
Technologies Used: Arduino Uno, HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor, L298N motor driver, DC motors.
Key Features:
Real-time distance measurement using the ultrasonic sensor for obstacle detection.
Basic path-following and obstacle-avoidance logic implemented in Arduino embedded C.
Receives "start patrol" / "stop patrol" commands from the Perception module; sends motor speed/direction commands; shares distance readings across the module interface.
This module forms the mobility backbone of the robot, ensuring safe, autonomous movement within its operating environment.
Module 2: Perception & Alerting System
Technologies Used: Raspberry Pi, PIR motion sensor, Pi Camera module, Python, Paho-MQTT client library, Mosquitto broker.
Key Features:
Continuously monitors the PIR sensor for motion events.
Triggers the camera to capture an image when motion is detected.
Publishes alert messages and image data to an MQTT broker, which forwards notifications to a cloud dashboard / user's mobile device.
Sends start/stop commands to the Navigation module to coordinate patrol behaviour.
This module gives the robot its "awareness", converting raw sensor events into actionable, remotely accessible alerts — a key demonstration of AI-and-IoT-enabled robotics.
Design Rationale
The two-module split was a deliberate design decision: separating time-critical, safety-relevant obstacle avoidance (Navigation, on the Arduino) from the computationally heavier, less time-critical perception and cloud communication (Perception & Alerting, on the Raspberry Pi) meant that a delay or failure in the AI/IoT layer would never compromise the robot's basic ability to avoid collisions. This separation of concerns, learned through the internship's system-architecture module, mirrors how safety-critical and intelligence layers are decoupled in real industrial and consumer robotic systems.
ANALYSIS
This section presents a SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats) of the capstone project "Smart IoT-Enabled Security & Navigation Robot", evaluating its design, implementation, and problem-solving capability in the context of AI-and-IoT-enabled robotics.
Strengths
The capstone project and the internship's delivery model showed several clear strengths, summarised below.
Comprehensive core functionality: the system successfully integrates obstacle-avoidance navigation, motion-triggered perception, image capture, and cloud alerting in a single cooperating pair of modules.
Modular, well-defined architecture: the Navigation and Perception & Alerting modules have clearly specified responsibilities and interfaces, making the system easier to test, maintain, and extend.
Reliable real-time communication: MQTT's lightweight publish/subscribe model ensures low-latency alert delivery even on constrained hardware.
Hands-on hardware–software integration: the project demonstrates genuine embedded-systems skills spanning microcontroller programming, sensor/actuator interfacing, and cloud connectivity.
Scalable design: additional sensors or a third module (e.g., a mapping or voice-alert module) could be added without redesigning the existing modules.
Weaknesses
At the same time, working within a simulated/virtual internship format and a compressed eight-week timeline introduced some practical limitations, outlined below.
Limited onboard AI: perception logic relies on threshold-based sensor triggers rather than a trained ML model, restricting the system's ability to distinguish between genuine threats and false positives (e.g., pets, shadows).
No persistent data storage: sensor logs and captured images are not stored in a structured database, limiting historical analysis.
Basic user interface: alerts are delivered as raw MQTT messages; a polished mobile/web dashboard was outside the scope of the internship timeline.
Power dependency: continuous operation of the Raspberry Pi and motors requires a stable power supply, which was not optimized for battery efficiency.
Opportunities
The current design also opens up several natural directions for future enhancement, listed here.
Integrating a trained Machine Learning model (e.g., a lightweight CNN) for image-based intruder/object classification to reduce false alerts.
Adding cloud-based data logging and a dashboard (e.g., using Node-RED or a simple web app) for historical analytics and remote configuration.
Extending the Navigation module with SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) using the Gazebo/ROS skills gained during the internship.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The capstone project follows a layered architecture in which sensing, control, actuation, and IoT connectivity are cleanly separated, allowing the Navigation and Perception & Alerting modules to be developed, tested, and debugged independently before integration.
Fig. 1 — System Architecture: AI & IoT Enabled Robotic Platform
System Flow Description
The ultrasonic sensor continuously feeds distance data to the Arduino, which runs the navigation control loop.
The PIR sensor, camera, and DHT11 sensor feed the Raspberry Pi, which performs perception processing and AI-assisted analysis.
The Arduino and Raspberry Pi communicate over a serial/I2C link to exchange patrol start/stop commands and status.
The Raspberry Pi publishes alert messages and image data to an MQTT broker (Mosquitto), which forwards them to a cloud dashboard or the user's mobile device.
This layered, modular design ensures that a failure or delay in one layer (e.g., the cloud connection) does not stop the core navigation safety logic from functioning.
Each layer exposes a narrow, well-defined interface to its neighbours (sensor readings, serial commands, MQTT topics), so a component can be swapped or upgraded - e.g. replacing the ultrasonic sensor with a LiDAR module - without redesigning the rest of the system.
The layered view also doubled as the internship's debugging map: whenever an issue appeared, it could be isolated to a single layer (sensing, control, actuation, or cloud) before being traced further, which considerably shortened debugging time during integration.

2. Navigation Module — Ultrasonic Obstacle Avoidance (Arduino, C++)
File: navigation_module.ino — reads distance from the HC-SR04 sensor and drives the DC motors via the L298N driver to avoid obstacles.
#define TRIG_PIN 9
#define ECHO_PIN 10
#define MOTOR_IN1 5
#define MOTOR_IN2 6
#define SAFE_DISTANCE_CM 20

bool patrolActive = false;

void setup() {
 pinMode(TRIG_PIN, OUTPUT);
 pinMode(ECHO_PIN, INPUT);
 pinMode(MOTOR_IN1, OUTPUT);
 pinMode(MOTOR_IN2, OUTPUT);
 Serial.begin(9600); // command link to Raspberry Pi
}

long readDistanceCM() {
 digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW); delayMicroseconds(2);
 digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, HIGH); delayMicroseconds(10);
 digitalWrite(TRIG_PIN, LOW);
 long duration = pulseIn(ECHO_PIN, HIGH);
 return duration * 0.034 / 2; // convert to centimetres
}

void loop() {
 if (Serial.available()) {
 String cmd = Serial.readStringUntil('\n');
 if (cmd == "START_PATROL") patrolActive = true;
 if (cmd == "STOP_PATROL") patrolActive = false;
 }
 if (patrolActive) {
 long distance = readDistanceCM();
 if (distance < SAFE_DISTANCE_CM) {
 stopMotors(); turnRight(300); // obstacle avoidance manoeuvre
 } else {
 moveForward();
 }
 } else {
 stopMotors();
 }
}
3. Perception & Alerting Module — Motion Detection & MQTT Publish (Raspberry Pi, Python)
File: perception_module.py — monitors the PIR sensor, captures an image on motion detection, and publishes an alert to the MQTT broker.
import RPi.GPIO as GPIO
import time
from picamera import PiCamera
import paho.mqtt.client as mqtt

PIR_PIN = 17
BROKER = "broker.local"
TOPIC_ALERT = "security/robot/alert"

GPIO.setmode(GPIO.BCM)
GPIO.setup(PIR_PIN, GPIO.IN)
camera = PiCamera()
client = mqtt.Client()
client.connect(BROKER, 1883, 60)

def publish_alert(image_path):
 client.publish(TOPIC_ALERT, f"Motion detected! Image: {image_path}")
 print("Alert published to MQTT broker.")

try:
 client.publish("security/robot/command", "START_PATROL")
 while True:
 if GPIO.input(PIR_PIN):
 filename = f"/home/pi/captures/motion_{int(time.time())}.jpg"
 camera.capture(filename)
 publish_alert(filename)
 time.sleep(5) # debounce interval
 time.sleep(0.2)
except KeyboardInterrupt:
 client.publish("security/robot/command", "STOP_PATROL")
 GPIO.cleanup()
4. Module Interface — MQTT Topic Design
The two modules were designed to remain loosely coupled, communicating only through a small, well-defined set of MQTT topics and serial commands:
security/robot/command -> "START_PATROL" | "STOP_PATROL" (Perception -> Navigation)
security/robot/alert -> "Motion detected! Image: <path>" (Perception -> Broker -> Dashboard)
security/robot/status -> "OBSTACLE_AVOIDED" | "PATROL_OK" (Navigation -> Broker -> Dashboard)
5. Dashboard Alert Payload — JSON Message Format
To keep the remote dashboard decoupled from the internal sensor logic, every MQTT alert message was serialised as a compact JSON object before being published to the security/robot/alert topic. This gave the dashboard a stable, self-describing message format regardless of future changes to the underlying sensor code:
{
 "device_id": "robot-01",
 "event": "motion_detected",
 "timestamp": "2026-07-14T10:22:41Z",
 "image_path": "/home/pi/captures/motion_1752488561.jpg",
 "confidence": "high",
 "navigation_state": "PATROL_OK",
 "battery_level_pct": 78
}
This structure meant the dashboard only needed to parse a single, versioned JSON schema, while the Navigation and Perception & Alerting modules on the robot side remained free to evolve their internal logic independently.
